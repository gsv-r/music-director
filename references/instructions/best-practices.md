# Short guide to what improves the result

Read this before generating. It contains the criteria that change the delivered
text. Everything else in the project is support for these decisions.

The final test for any rule: does it change the lyrics, the style, the title or
the user's understanding of the result? If not, leave it out of the answer.

## Lyrics

- Write in the language of the brief unless the user asks for another. Preserve
  that language's natural stress, syllabic rhythm and pronunciation.
- Structure follows the song, not a default template. Use section markers for
  the parts that exist; order and length come from the brief and the idea.
- Keep lines short enough to sing in one breath. Check stress by speaking and
  then singing the line slowly: if the tongue trips, the word is too long, or
  the accent falls wrong, simplify it.
- Make each line follow from the previous one. Prefer one concrete, observable
  image over an abstract statement of feeling. Verses carry detail; the chorus
  carries the "why" when the song has one.
- Give the hook a short, memorable phrase, set apart by rhythm, pitch, pause or
  position. Repetition is useful when it carries the central idea; change a
  returning line only when the change means something.
- Define who sings to whom when the writing needs it, and keep that point of
  view consistent. Abstract, chant and texture-based writing need no narrator.
- Rhyme when it comes naturally. Prefer slant and internal rhyme to mechanical
  AABB with equal-length lines. Never force a word only to close a rhyme.
- Avoid filler and ready-made phrases ("in the night", "feel alive", "you're my
  everything", "love is a fire"). Replace them with a specific detail from
  this song. Avoid philosophical language and puzzles that obscure the feeling.
- Put short vocal interjections (one to three syllables) in the lyric field
  only when the genre and the moment support them. A clean song is valid.
- Section marker format: `[Section - audible indication, audible indication]`,
  up to three indications. Describe what is heard in that passage, not the
  global sound and not a generic instruction. Keep sung text and instructions
  visually separate.

## Style

- One concise line built from the brief, in this order: genre or subgenre;
  tempo or feel with BPM when useful; two or three main instruments with their
  function; the voice as timbre plus way of singing; audible production
  texture; vocal diction and language. Add global movement only when it changes
  the result.
- Use audible, specific language: "dry close vocal", "brushed drums",
  "warm sub-bass", "sparse piano". Replace vague adjectives such as "epic",
  "beautiful", "cinematic" or "polished" with the concrete traits that produce
  them, or drop them.
- Name a groove and a drum feel alongside the BPM when the genre calls for it.
  Do not add genres, instruments or eras the user did not ask for; combine at
  most two or three styles around one anchor.
- Describe the singer, never who the singer is: timbre, delivery and technique
  only when it is heard. No artist, band or song names.
- No tool parameters in the line: no percentages, dB, ms or Hz, no structured
  data and no tool-specific tags.
- Exclusions are optional. If used, keep two or three at the end of the line.
- Four to seven specific descriptors is a heuristic for density, not a target.

## Title

One to five words in the language of the lyrics, tied to the hook or the
central idea. No brackets, no explanation. It is identification, not a fifth
musical dimension.

## Analysis

Write short prose for the user, after the three blocks. Say what the piece
proposes, what the lyrics are about, how the emotion moves and why the style
fits. Base every claim on the delivered text or the brief. Mark interpretation
as possibility ("may suggest", "one reading is"). Do not invent events,
speakers or justifications, do not reproduce the lyrics, do not restate the
style line or the section marks, and do not expose private reasoning. A short
song gets a short analysis.

## Final check

Read the lyrics aloud once. Look for a line that does not sing, a mark in the
wrong place, a vague style adjective, a contradiction with the brief, or a
title unrelated to the song. Fix those in one pass. Report what cannot be fixed
in the analysis; do not keep rewriting.
