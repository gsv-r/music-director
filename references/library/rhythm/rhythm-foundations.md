# Rhythm & Groove — Reference Library

This file is part of the Music Director `references/library/`. It provides **reference knowledge**
about rhythmic and metric phenomena: meter, swing and straight feel, shuffle,
backbeat, syncopation, groove, and rhythmic density. It does not contain
orchestration rules; those remain in `references/instructions/style.md`,
`references/instructions/arrangement.md`, `references/instructions/lyrics.md`, and
`references/instructions/performance.md`.

Scope note: this file covers rhythmic theory and groove phenomena only. Formal
architecture belongs to `references/library/arrangement/`. Vocal timing interpretation belongs
to `references/library/performance/`. Lyric prosody belongs to Lyrics. Global sonic identity
belongs to Style. Production mechanisms belong to `references/library/production/`.

Consult via `references/library/index.md`: locate the term/alias/intent, then read only this
file (or the relevant section).

### Simple and compound meter

- **Term:** Simple and compound meter
- **Aliases:** simple meter, compound meter, binary meter, ternary meter
- **Category:** rhythm/meter
- **Definition:** In simple meter, each beat divides into two (binary) equal subdivisions; in compound meter, each beat divides into three (ternary) equal subdivisions. The time signature's grouping indicates which applies.
- **Audible effect:** Simple meter feels even and march-like; compound meter feels lilting or grouped in threes.
- **When to use:** When the intended feel requires binary or ternary beat division; when grouping supports the composition's pulse.
- **When to avoid / limitations:** When the intended feel does not match the chosen division; when compound meter is used without a genuine ternary beat feel. Avoid assuming simple meter is the default.
- **Compatible combinations:** Backbeat, syncopation, rhythmic density.
- **Conflicts:** Mismatched subdivision feel; forced ternary feel in a binary context.
- **Relation to other concepts:** Meter is a formal/structural property; Arrangement decides metric changes as formal events. Rhythm defines the division; Arrangement materializes it.
- **Knowledge type:** consolidated.
- **Source:** London, J. (2012). *Hearing in Time: Psychological Aspects of Musical Meter* (2nd ed.). Oxford University Press; Cooper, G. W., & Meyer, L. B. (1960). *The Rhythmic Structure of Music*. University of Chicago Press; Temperley, D. (2001). *The Cognition of Basic Musical Structures*. MIT Press.

### Swing and straight feel

- **Term:** Swing and straight feel
- **Aliases:** swing feel, straight feel, swung feel, even subdivision
- **Category:** rhythm/groove
- **Definition:** Swing feel is an uneven subdivision of beats, typically a longer downbeat followed by a shorter upbeat, with a ratio that varies with tempo, performer, and style. Straight feel is an even subdivision at the relevant rhythmic level. Both can produce groove.
- **Audible effect:** Swing has a lilting, propulsive quality; straight feels even, locked, and direct.
- **When to use:** Swing when a lilting, jazz-derived feel serves the composition; straight when even subdivision serves the composition.
- **When to avoid / limitations:** When the chosen feel conflicts with the genre or intent. Do not impose a universal swing ratio (commonly cited as 2:1 but empirically variable). Do not treat straight feel as absence of groove.
- **Compatible combinations:** Backbeat, syncopation, groove, shuffle.
- **Conflicts:** Imposing swing on a straight context (or vice versa) without intent; treating 2:1 as a fixed rule.
- **Relation to other concepts:** A rhythmic feel, not a harmonic or formal device. Performance interprets timing; Rhythm defines the feel. Tradition variation is documented rather than collapsed.
- **Knowledge type:** consolidated (jazz studies); convention (genre-specific usage).
- **Variation across traditions/sources:** Swing ratio varies with tempo and context (Friberg & Sundström, 2002); jazz microrhythm is context-dependent (Benadon, 2006). The perceived "swing" is not a single fixed ratio.
- **Source:** Friberg, A., & Sundström, A. (2002). "Swing Ratios and Ensemble Timing in Jazz Performance: Evidence for a Common Rhythmic Pattern." *Music Perception*, 19(3), 333–349; Butterfield, M. W. (2006). "The Power of Anacrusis: Engendered Feeling in Groove-Based Musics." *Music Theory Online*, 12(4); Butterfield, M. W. (2011). "Why Do Jazz Musicians Swing Their Eighth Notes?" *Music Theory Spectrum*, 33(1), 3–26; Benadon, F. (2006). "Slicing the Beat: Jazz Eighth-Notes as Expressive Microrhythm." *Ethnomusicology*, 50(1), 73–98.

### Shuffle

- **Term:** Shuffle
- **Aliases:** shuffle feel, triplet shuffle, swung sixteenths (related)
- **Category:** rhythm/groove
- **Definition:** A groove commonly associated with triplet-derived subdivision, but historically and stylistically variable. It is not universally defined as swung sixteenth notes. A general swing feel and a specific shuffle groove are distinct.
- **Audible effect:** A loping, triplet-derived bounce, or a straighter 16th feel depending on tradition and performance.
- **When to use:** When a shuffle-derived groove serves the composition's genre and intent.
- **When to avoid / limitations:** When the composition calls for a general swing feel rather than a shuffle; when the shuffle is assumed to be a single fixed pattern. Do not universalize the historical trajectory from shuffle/triplet to straight subdivisions.
- **Compatible combinations:** Swing and straight feel, backbeat, groove.
- **Conflicts:** Conflating shuffle with general swing; assuming one fixed shuffle pattern.
- **Relation to other concepts:** A specific groove, not a synonym for swing. Performance interprets execution; Rhythm defines the phenomenon.
- **Knowledge type:** consolidated (as association); convention (historical and stylistic variation).
- **Variation across traditions/sources:** Shuffle has varied across blues, rock, jazz, and funk traditions. Stewart (2000) documents a historically situated transition toward straighter subdivisions in American popular music, which is not a universal trajectory.
- **Source:** Benadon, F. (2006). "Slicing the Beat: Jazz Eighth-Notes as Expressive Microrhythm." *Ethnomusicology*, 50(1), 73–98; Iyer, V. (2002). "Embodied Mind, Situated Cognition, and Expressive Microtiming in African-American Music." *Music Perception*, 19(3), 387–414; Stewart, A. (2000). "'Funky Drummer': New Orleans, James Brown and the Rhythmic Transformation of American Popular Music." *Popular Music*, 19(3), 293–318.

### Backbeat

- **Term:** Backbeat
- **Aliases:** back-beat, snare on 2 and 4
- **Category:** rhythm/accentuation
- **Definition:** Emphasis on beats 2 and 4 (in 4/4) or equivalent offbeat positions, creating a characteristic groove. Metric accent theory explains the hierarchy; the backbeat itself is a specific musical practice with cultural and historical context.
- **Audible effect:** A strong, propulsive emphasis on the offbeat quarter-note positions, driving the groove.
- **When to use:** When the genre and intent call for a backbeat-driven groove.
- **When to avoid / limitations:** When the genre or intent does not use a backbeat (e.g., compound-meter or jazz contexts). Do not present the backbeat as universal across genres.
- **Compatible combinations:** Straight feel, groove, rhythmic density.
- **Conflicts:** Compound or ternary meters; genres where backbeat is not practiced.
- **Relation to other concepts:** Metric accent theory (Cooper & Meyer) is general; the backbeat as practice is culturally and historically situated (Baur). Rhythm defines the phenomenon; Arrangement decides accent placement.
- **Knowledge type:** consolidated (metric accent theory); convention (musical practice and cultural context).
- **Source:** Cooper, G. W., & Meyer, L. B. (1960). *The Rhythmic Structure of Music*. University of Chicago Press; Baur, S. (2021). "Towards a Cultural History of the Backbeat." In *The Cambridge Companion to the Drum Kit* (pp. 34–51). Cambridge University Press.

### Syncopation

- **Term:** Syncopation
- **Aliases:** syncopated accent, displaced accent
- **Category:** rhythm/syncopation
- **Definition:** Displacement of an accent to a weak beat or offbeat, creating tension against the established pulse. Distinct from general rhythmic displacement, which may not involve accent.
- **Audible effect:** A sense of rhythmic tension, surprise, or forward lean against the pulse.
- **When to use:** When rhythmic tension and release against the pulse serve the composition.
- **When to avoid / limitations:** When the composition requires a stable, unaccented pulse; when syncopation obscures the meter. Avoid overuse that erodes the sense of pulse.
- **Compatible combinations:** Swing and straight feel, backbeat, groove, rhythmic density.
- **Conflicts:** Stable, unaccented pulse; metric clarity as the primary goal.
- **Relation to other concepts:** A rhythmic phenomenon defined here; Arrangement decides density and placement; Performance interprets execution. Distinct from general displacement.
- **Knowledge type:** consolidated.
- **Source:** Cooper, G. W., & Meyer, L. B. (1960). *The Rhythmic Structure of Music*. University of Chicago Press.

### Groove

- **Term:** Groove
- **Aliases:** groove feel, rhythmic groove
- **Category:** rhythm/groove
- **Definition:** An interaction of rhythmic patterns, performer and layer interaction, timing and microtiming, listener experience (movement, immersion, participation), and cultural and stylistic context. Multiple definitions exist in the literature; no single universal formulation is imposed.
- **Audible effect:** A sense of rhythmic cohesion, momentum, and bodily engagement; the music "locks" or "feels good."
- **When to use:** When describing or shaping the global rhythmic identity and the interaction of rhythmic layers.
- **When to avoid / limitations:** When a single, precise technical claim is required; groove is a multidimensional concept and resists reduction to one factor (including microtiming alone). Avoid presenting one theoretical perspective as the definitive definition.
- **Compatible combinations:** Swing and straight feel, shuffle, backbeat, syncopation, rhythmic density.
- **Conflicts:** Reducing groove to a single factor; treating groove as a fixed, universal pattern.
- **Relation to other concepts:** A rhythmic phenomenon and listener experience; Style owns global groove; Arrangement owns layer interaction; Performance owns timing interpretation. Keil (1987) and Iyer (2002) are specific theoretical perspectives; Duman et al. (2023) provides a multidimensional participatory-experience framework.
- **Knowledge type:** interpretation (multiple perspectives); convention (stylistic usage).
- **Variation across traditions/sources:** Groove is theorized differently across traditions (participatory discrepancies, embodied cognition, multidimensional participatory experience). Document the variation rather than collapsing it.
- **Source:** Keil, C. (1987). "Participatory Discrepancies and the Power of Music." *Cultural Anthropology*, 2(3), 275-283; Iyer, V. (2002). "Embodied Mind, Situated Cognition, and Expressive Microtiming in African-American Music." *Music Perception*, 19(3), 387-414; Duman, D., Snape, N., Danso, A., Toiviainen, P., & Luck, G. (2024). "Groove as a Multidimensional Participatory Experience." *Psychology of Music*, 52(1), 93-116 (first published online 8 June 2023; DOI 10.1177/03057356231165327).

### Rhythmic density

- **Term:** Rhythmic density
- **Aliases:** rhythmic event density, rhythmic activity
- **Category:** rhythm/density
- **Definition:** Rhythmic event density is the frequency of distinct rhythmic events over a defined time interval. In the operational definition used by Senn et al. (2017), a distinct event is a metrical grid position with at least one note onset; rhythmic density is the mean number of distinct events per second, i.e., the reciprocal of the mean inter-onset interval. It is distinct from layer density (number and interaction of simultaneous layers) and from perceived energy (subjective musical experience). Where event density is quantified, the counting basis must be specified: the relevant time interval, the rhythmic part or aggregate texture, the treatment of simultaneous note onsets, and the event level being measured. Operational measures are context-specific and should not be treated as universal definitions of all musical density.
- **Audible effect:** High event density feels busy or active; low event density feels sparse or open. Perceived energy may not match event density.
- **When to use:** When describing or deciding the level of rhythmic activity in a section or composition.
- **When to avoid / limitations:** When layer count or perceived energy is the actual concern (use layer density or dynamic description instead). Operational measures (e.g., event counts) are context-specific, not universal definitions of all musical density. Do not treat the operational measure as a universal law.
- **Compatible combinations:** Syncopation, groove, simple and compound meter.
- **Conflicts:** Conflating event density with layer density or perceived energy.
- **Relation to other concepts:** Rhythm defines rhythmic event density. Arrangement owns layer density and the distribution of layers. Style owns global sonic identity. These three are not interchangeable.
- **Knowledge type:** consolidated (concept); interpretation (operational measures).
- **Source:** Cooper, G. W., & Meyer, L. B. (1960). *The Rhythmic Structure of Music*. University of Chicago Press; Temperley, D. (2001). *The Cognition of Basic Musical Structures*. MIT Press; Senn, O., Bullerjahn, C., Kilchenmann, L., & von Georgi, R. (2017). "Rhythmic Density Affects Listeners' Emotional Response to Microtiming." *Frontiers in Psychology*, 8, 1709 (DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01709).

## Domain boundaries (reminder)

- **Rhythm library**: rhythmic theory and groove phenomena; does not decide materialization (Arrangement), global identity (Style), timing interpretation (Performance), or prosodic fit (Lyrics).
- **Style**: global rhythmic identity and groove choice.
- **Arrangement**: distribution of rhythmic roles, layer density, metric/rhythmic changes as decisions.
- **Performance**: timing interpretation and expressive execution.
- **Lyrics**: syllabic stress, prosody, rhythmic fit.

Anti-duplication: no redefinition of phrasing-timing execution, section-development local execution, Style groove vocabulary, Arrangement density rules, or Lyrics prosody rules.

## Consultation policy (reminder)

- Style: targeted, when groove/tempo descriptors are material.
- Arrangement: targeted, when rhythmic event density, meter, syncopation, or rhythmic organization materially informs a decision (even without formal restructuring); not auto-triggered by layer-count changes alone.
- Performance: optional and targeted for timing feel; existing Performance consultation remains mandatory.
- Lyrics: optional and targeted when prosody makes rhythmic theory relevant.
- Analysis: does not consult.
