# Harmony and tonality reference library

This file is part of the Music Director `references/library/`. It provides reference
knowledge about harmonic and tonal phenomena: tonal center and key, scales
and modes, consonance/dissonance and chord color, harmonic function and
cadence, harmonic rhythm, modulation, non-functional and static harmony,
harmonic expectation, and the limits of Western tonal concepts across
traditions. It does not contain universal orchestration rules; those are canonical in
`references/instructions/orchestration.md`. Module-specific guidance remains in
`references/instructions/style.md`, `references/instructions/arrangement.md`,
`references/instructions/lyrics.md`, and `references/instructions/performance.md`.

Scope note: this file covers harmonic/tonal reference knowledge only. Formal
architecture belongs to `references/library/arrangement/`. Rhythmic theory belongs to
`references/library/rhythm/`. Production mechanisms belong to `references/library/production/`.
Vocal technique and interpretive evolution belong to
`references/library/performance/`. Global sonic identity belongs to Style. Lyric
prosody belongs to Lyrics.

Consult via `references/library/index.md`: locate the term/alias/intent, then read only
this file (or the relevant section).

### Tonal center, key and tonal ambiguity

- Term: Tonal center, key and tonal ambiguity
- Aliases: tonal center, key, key center, tonal ambiguity, tonal uncertainty, atonality (contrast, not synonym)
- Category: harmony/tonality
- Definition: In tonal music, a tonal center is the pitch or chord that functions as a point of orientation and stability; key describes the scale and center that organize pitch relations. Tonal ambiguity occurs when the music does not establish or sustain a single clear center.
- Audible effect: A clear key feels oriented and stable; ambiguity feels suspended, floating, unresolved, or non-tonal.
- When to use: When the global identity needs orientation; when ambiguity serves instability, openness, or non-tonal color.
- When to avoid / limitations: When ambiguity is unintentional; when a clear center is needed for prosodic or structural clarity. Do not treat a single tonal center as a universal requirement.
- Compatible combinations: Harmonic rhythm, harmonic function and cadence/resolution, scales and modes.
- Conflicts: Forced atonality in a strongly tonal context without intent; imposing a tonal center on music that does not use one.
- Relation to other concepts: Style owns the global tonal center; Arrangement owns changes of center as formal events.
- Knowledge type: consolidated (tonal center perception); interpretation (tonal ambiguity as condition).
- Variation across traditions/sources: Western tonal theory is one documented family of practices; other traditions may organize pitch without a single hierarchical center.
- Source: Krumhansl, C. L. (1990). *Cognitive Foundations of Musical Pitch*. Oxford University Press; Lerdahl, F., & Jackendoff, R. (1983). *A Generative Theory of Tonal Music*. MIT Press; Temperley, D. (2001). *The Cognition of Basic Musical Structures*. MIT Press.

### Scales and modes

- Term: Scales and modes
- Aliases: scale, mode, diatonic scale, modal scale, non-diatonic scale
- Category: harmony/scale
- Definition: A scale is an ordered set of pitch classes used as a resource; a mode is a particular organization or emphasis within such a set, often with characteristic melodic and harmonic behavior.
- Audible effect: Scale and mode shape melodic color, available sonorities, and tonal character.
- When to use: When global color, melodic resource, or modal flavor is material to the composition.
- When to avoid / limitations: When a mode is used as a label without audible consequence; when non-diatonic systems are reduced to diatonic equivalents. Do not treat Western diatonic modes as universal.
- Compatible combinations: Tonal center, key and tonal ambiguity; consonance, dissonance, chord color and modal interchange.
- Conflicts: Treating a mode as a mere scale without functional or melodic implications.
- Relation to other concepts: Style owns the global scale/mode; Lyrics and Performance may use it as topline color.
- Knowledge type: consolidated (scale construction); convention (modal usage in documented styles).
- Variation across traditions/sources: Western diatonic modes are one documented family of scale practice; broader cross-tradition comparison requires tradition-specific sources.
- Source: Krumhansl, C. L. (1990). *Cognitive Foundations of Musical Pitch*. Oxford University Press; Nettl, B. (2005/2015). *The Study of Ethnomusicology* (methodological caution; not detailed pitch systems).

### Consonance, dissonance, chord color and modal interchange

- Term: Consonance, dissonance, chord color and modal interchange
- Aliases: consonance, dissonance, chord color, chord quality, modal interchange, borrowed chords
- Category: harmony/color
- Definition: Consonance and dissonance describe degrees of stability or tension among simultaneous or successive pitches. Chord color arises from chord quality, extensions, alterations, and non-diatonic sonorities. Modal interchange borrows sonorities from a related mode, often the parallel mode, to add color. It is a coloristic resource and does not necessarily change the tonal center; in specific contexts it may also acquire functional implications, which are treated separately under harmonic function.
- Audible effect: Consonance sounds stable or resolved; dissonance sounds tense or unstable; color changes the harmonic hue; modal interchange adds an unexpected coloristic deviation.
- When to use: When stability, tension, or color is material; when a coloristic deviation from the prevailing mode is desired.
- When to avoid / limitations: When color is added without purpose; when dissonance is unprepared or unresolved without intent; when modal interchange is treated as an automatic color recipe. Do not present modal interchange as a universal explanation of harmonic color.
- Compatible combinations: Tonal center, key and tonal ambiguity; harmonic function and cadence/resolution; modulation (as a distinct process).
- Conflicts: Conflating borrowed color with modulation or with a redefinition of function.
- Relation to other concepts: Style owns global color; Arrangement may use color per section; Performance interprets tension.
- Knowledge type: consolidated (interval and chord structure); convention (color usage and modal interchange); interpretation (perceptual consonance/dissonance effects).
- Variation across traditions/sources: Perceptions of consonance, dissonance, and color vary by tradition, tuning, and context.
- Source: Krumhansl, C. L. (1990). *Cognitive Foundations of Musical Pitch*. Oxford University Press; Huron, D. (2006). *Sweet Anticipation: Music and the Psychology of Expectation*. MIT Press; Tymoczko, D. (2011). *A Geometry of Music: Harmony and Counterpoint in the Extended Common Practice*. Oxford University Press; Aldwell, E., Schachter, C., & Cadwallader, A. (2018). *Harmony and Voice Leading* (5th ed.). Cengage; Piston, W. (1987). *Harmony* (5th ed., rev. DeVoto). Norton.

### Harmonic function and cadence/resolution

- Term: Harmonic function and cadence/resolution
- Aliases: harmonic function, tonic, subdominant, dominant, cadence, resolution, functional harmony
- Category: harmony/function
- Definition: Harmonic function describes the role of chords in establishing, destabilizing, and resolving tonality. Cadences are points of arrival or suspension that may confirm, delay, or avoid resolution. Functional categories are a documented Western tonal practice, not a universal harmonic logic.
- Audible effect: Functional progressions create directed motion; cadences create arrival, suspension, or avoidance.
- When to use: When directed harmonic motion or arrival is material to the composition.
- When to avoid / limitations: When function is assumed to be universal; when a cadence is used as a formula; when non-functional color or stasis is intended.
- Compatible combinations: Tonal center, key and tonal ambiguity; harmonic rhythm; modulation.
- Conflicts: Treating functional harmony as the only valid harmonic logic.
- Relation to other concepts: Arrangement owns cadential arrivals as formal events; Style owns global functional character.
- Knowledge type: consolidated (functional theory); convention (cadential practice); interpretation (resolution effect).
- Variation across traditions/sources: Functional categories are tradition-specific; not all music uses tonic-subdominant-dominant logic.
- Source: Aldwell, E., Schachter, C., & Cadwallader, A. (2018). *Harmony and Voice Leading* (5th ed.). Cengage; Schoenberg, A. (1978). *Theory of Harmony* (trans. Roy E. Carter). University of California Press.

### Harmonic rhythm

- Term: Harmonic rhythm
- Aliases: harmonic rhythm, rate of harmonic change, harmonic density
- Category: harmony/rhythm
- Definition: Harmonic rhythm is the rate and pattern of chord or harmonic change relative to meter, phrase, and form.
- Audible effect: Slow harmonic rhythm feels stable or spacious; fast harmonic rhythm feels active or propulsive.
- When to use: When the rate of harmonic change affects density, form, or global movement.
- When to avoid / limitations: When harmonic rhythm is confused with rhythmic event density, which belongs to the Rhythm domain.
- Compatible combinations: Tonal center, key and tonal ambiguity; harmonic function and cadence/resolution; form.
- Conflicts: Conflating harmonic rhythm with meter or groove.
- Relation to other concepts: Arrangement owns harmonic rhythm as a formal/density factor; Style owns global harmonic rhythm.
- Knowledge type: consolidated (harmonic rhythm as structural phenomenon); convention (stylistic usage).
- Variation across traditions/sources: Acceptable rates of harmonic change vary by style and tradition.
- Source: Lerdahl, F., & Jackendoff, R. (1983). *A Generative Theory of Tonal Music*. MIT Press; Temperley, D. (2001). *The Cognition of Basic Musical Structures*. MIT Press; Aldwell, E., Schachter, C., & Cadwallader, A. (2018). *Harmony and Voice Leading* (5th ed.). Cengage.

### Modulation

- Term: Modulation
- Aliases: modulation, key change, tonal shift
- Category: harmony/modulation
- Definition: Modulation is a process that establishes a new tonal center. A mere change of tonality or color may not constitute modulation.
- Audible effect: Modulation creates a sense of relocation, lift, or formal turn.
- When to use: When a new center is established and serves form or global trajectory.
- When to avoid / limitations: When every coloristic deviation is called modulation; when modulation is used as a default lift.
- Compatible combinations: Tonal center, key and tonal ambiguity; harmonic function and cadence/resolution; form.
- Conflicts: Conflating modulation with modal interchange or tonicization.
- Relation to other concepts: Arrangement owns modulation as a formal event; Style owns the global modulation plan.
- Knowledge type: convention (modulatory practice); interpretation (formal effect).
- Variation across traditions/sources: Modulatory practices and their formal meanings vary by style and tradition.
- Source: Aldwell, E., Schachter, C., & Cadwallader, A. (2018). *Harmony and Voice Leading* (5th ed.). Cengage; Schoenberg, A. (1978). *Theory of Harmony* (trans. Roy E. Carter). University of California Press; Lerdahl, F., & Jackendoff, R. (1983). *A Generative Theory of Tonal Music*. MIT Press.

### Non-functional, static harmony, pedal and drone

- Term: Non-functional, static harmony, pedal and drone
- Aliases: non-functional harmony, static harmony, pedal point, drone, planing, ostinato harmony
- Category: harmony/static
- Definition: Non-functional harmony organizes pitch without directed functional motion. Static harmony, pedal points, and drones sustain or repeat a tonal reference, creating color, stasis, or tension.
- Audible effect: Stasis, suspension, color, or tension without conventional resolution.
- When to use: When stability of reference, color, or suspension is desired.
- When to avoid / limitations: When stasis is unintended. Do not make unsupported acoustic or psychoacoustic claims about drones or pedals.
- Compatible combinations: Tonal center, key and tonal ambiguity; consonance, dissonance, chord color and modal interchange; texture.
- Conflicts: Treating non-functional harmony as an absence of structure.
- Relation to other concepts: Style owns global static color; Arrangement owns pedal/drone as layer or formal event.
- Knowledge type: convention (pedal/drone practice and sustained-tone reference as tonal anchor).
- Variation across traditions/sources: Drone and pedal practices vary widely across traditions.
- Source: Tymoczko, D. (2011). *A Geometry of Music: Harmony and Counterpoint in the Extended Common Practice*. Oxford University Press; Aldwell, E., Schachter, C., & Cadwallader, A. (2018). *Harmony and Voice Leading* (5th ed.). Cengage; Schoenberg, A. (1978). *Theory of Harmony* (trans. Roy E. Carter). University of California Press.

### Harmonic expectation, tension and release

- Term: Harmonic expectation, tension and release
- Aliases: harmonic expectation, tension and release, implication and realization
- Category: harmony/expectation
- Definition: Harmonic expectation describes how listeners anticipate continuation and resolution. Tension and release arise from the relationship between anticipated and actual harmonic events.
- Audible effect: Anticipation creates forward motion; fulfilled or denied expectation creates resolution, surprise, or instability.
- When to use: When emotional or formal trajectory depends on anticipation.
- When to avoid / limitations: When expectation is treated as universal; when tension is asserted without observable support.
- Compatible combinations: Tonal center, key and tonal ambiguity; harmonic function and cadence/resolution; modulation.
- Conflicts: Conflating harmonic expectation with formal climax or performance dynamics.
- Relation to other concepts: Performance interprets tension; Lyrics aligns prosody; Arrangement owns formal tension.
- Knowledge type: interpretation (expectation/tension); consolidated (empirical expectation studies).
- Variation across traditions/sources: Expectations vary by listener enculturation and tradition.
- Source: Meyer, L. B. (1956). *Emotion and Meaning in Music*. University of Chicago Press; Huron, D. (2006). *Sweet Anticipation: Music and the Psychology of Expectation*. MIT Press; Krumhansl, C. L. (1990). *Cognitive Foundations of Musical Pitch*. Oxford University Press; Lerdahl, F., & Jackendoff, R. (1983). *A Generative Theory of Tonal Music*. MIT Press.

### Pitch organization across musical traditions

- Term: Pitch organization across musical traditions
- Aliases: cross-cultural pitch organization, non-Western tonality, tonal universals (caution)
- Category: harmony/methodology
- Definition: A methodological caution: Western tonal concepts (center, function, cadence, modulation) describe one documented family of practices and should not be assumed to fit all musical traditions.
- Audible effect: Not a sound; a limit on how harmonic concepts may be applied.
- When to use: When a composition draws on non-Western or mixed traditions; when avoiding universalist assumptions.
- When to avoid / limitations: As a source of positive instructions for representing specific traditions; as a substitute for tradition-specific sources. Do not generalize from Nettl or Arom beyond their documented scope.
- Compatible combinations: Tonal center, key and tonal ambiguity; scales and modes.
- Relation to other concepts: All modules should treat this entry as a methodological caution, not a recipe: it limits the application of Western tonal concepts and does not provide positive instructions for representing specific traditions.
- Knowledge type: convention (documented practice in specific traditions); interpretation (limits of application of Western concepts).
- Variation across traditions/sources: Documented practices vary; no single cross-tradition system is established here.
- Source: Nettl, B. (2005/2015). *The Study of Ethnomusicology* (methodological caution); Arom, S. (1991). *African Polyphony and Polyrhythm* (specific African practice); Krumhansl, C. L. (1990). *Cognitive Foundations of Musical Pitch* (perceptual data in selected repertoires).
