# Form & Structure — Reference Library (Arrangement)

This file is part of the Music Director `references/library/`. It provides **reference knowledge**
about formal architecture: transitions as formal events, climax preparation and
release, delayed resolution, formal contrast, repetition with formal variation, and
non-conventional forms. It does not contain universal orchestration rules; those are canonical in
`references/instructions/orchestration.md`. Module-specific guidance remains in
`references/instructions/arrangement.md`.

Scope note: this file covers formal architecture only. Metric and rhythmic theory
belongs to `references/library/rhythm/`. Production mechanisms belong to
`references/library/production/`. Vocal interpretation and interpretive evolution belong to
`references/library/performance/`. Lyric content and verbal variation belong to Lyrics.
Global sonic identity belongs to Style.

Consult via `references/library/index.md`: locate the term/alias/intent, then read only this
file (or the relevant section).

### Transition as formal event

- **Term:** Transition as formal event
- **Aliases:** formal transition, transition event, sectional transition
- **Category:** arrangement/form-structure
- **Definition:** A change between sections or passages that alters the perceived form, balance, or trajectory of the composition, rather than merely connecting two equivalent passages.
- **Audible effect:** A perceptible shift in direction — the listener senses that the music has moved to a new formal state, not just continued.
- **When to use:** When a change (metric, tonal, textural, or layer-based) materially affects the form, balance, or development of the composition and should be heard as a structural event.
- **When to avoid / limitations:** When the change is cosmetic or connecting only; labeling every small change as a formal event inflates the structure and obscures genuine architecture. Avoid when continuity is the intended effect.
- **Compatible combinations:** Formal contrast, climax preparation, delayed resolution.
- **Conflicts:** Unmarked, seamless continuity (when continuity is the intent).
- **Relation to other concepts:** Distinguished from a simple connector or fill; a formal transition is a decision about form, not about execution. Metric theory (Rhythm library) describes the change; Arrangement decides whether it becomes a formal event. Caplin's framework addresses transitions within classical form; applying the concept to other traditions is an interpretive application, not a universally established rule.
- **Knowledge type:** consolidated (classical-form context); interpretation (generalization beyond that tradition).
- **Source:** Caplin, W. E. (1998). *Classical Form: A Theory of Formal Functions for the Instrumental Music of Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven*. Oxford University Press.

### Climax preparation

- **Term:** Climax preparation
- **Aliases:** formal build, peak preparation, tension preparation
- **Category:** arrangement/form-structure
- **Definition:** The formal process by which a composition accumulates layers, register, density, and/or space so that a subsequent peak is prepared rather than abrupt.
- **Audible effect:** Growing weight, height, or pressure before the peak; the listener anticipates a release.
- **When to use:** When a peak or release needs to feel earned; when accumulation (of layers, register, or density) serves the form.
- **When to avoid / limitations:** When immediacy is the intent; when accumulation would dilute a sudden impact. Avoid mechanical layer-stacking without a musical reason. Avoid assuming a peak must be prepared (a sudden climax is valid).
- **Compatible combinations:** Delayed resolution, formal contrast, climax release and resolution.
- **Conflicts:** Sudden, unprepared climax (when that is the intent); flat dynamics.
- **Relation to other concepts:** Preparation is the formal counterpart to interpretive tension (Performance library); Arrangement decides the formal accumulation, Performance decides the interpretive intensity.
- **Knowledge type:** consolidated.
- **Source:** Meyer, L. B. (1956). *Emotion and Meaning in Music*. University of Chicago Press; Lerdahl, F., & Jackendoff, R. (1983). *A Generative Theory of Tonal Music*. MIT Press.

### Climax release and resolution

- **Term:** Climax release and resolution
- **Aliases:** peak release, formal resolution, climax resolution
- **Category:** arrangement/form-structure
- **Definition:** The formal event in which a prepared (or unprepared) peak is released or resolved, through contrast, withdrawal, or arrival, and the composition moves to a new state.
- **Audible effect:** A sense of arrival, release, or transformation after tension or density; the music "lands" somewhere new.
- **When to use:** When a peak needs a clear consequence; when release or arrival serves the form.
- **When to avoid / limitations:** When restraint or non-resolution is the intent; when a release would undercut an unresolved ending. Restraint and non-resolution are valid; a climax need not resolve triumphantly.
- **Compatible combinations:** Climax preparation, formal contrast, delayed resolution.
- **Conflicts:** Unresolved endings, intentional non-resolution, restraint throughout.
- **Relation to other concepts:** Distinguished from interpretive "final surge" (Performance library), which describes execution intensity; this entry describes the formal consequence of a peak.
- **Knowledge type:** consolidated.
- **Source:** Meyer, L. B. (1956). *Emotion and Meaning in Music*. University of Chicago Press.

### Delayed resolution and suspension

- **Term:** Delayed resolution and suspension
- **Aliases:** formal suspension, delayed resolution, prolonged instability
- **Category:** arrangement/form-structure
- **Definition:** The deliberate prolongation of an unresolved or unstable formal state before a release or arrival, sustaining expectation.
- **Audible effect:** Heightened anticipation; the listener waits for a resolution that is withheld.
- **When to use:** When expectation and anticipation serve the composition; when a release should feel earned by waiting.
- **When to avoid / limitations:** When patience is not available in the form (short durations); when withholding becomes frustrating rather than engaging. Avoid overuse, which can feel manipulative. Avoid claiming harmonic mechanisms not present — this entry concerns formal delay, not harmonic suspension theory.
- **Compatible combinations:** Climax preparation, climax release and resolution, formal contrast.
- **Conflicts:** Immediate resolution, rapid pacing, stable/static forms where no expectation is built.
- **Relation to other concepts:** A formal device; distinct from harmonic suspension, which is a specific harmonic and voice-leading device involving preparation, dissonance, and resolution (not covered here). Related to expectation psychology in music.
- **Knowledge type:** consolidated (formal device); interpretation (psychological effect).
- **Variation across traditions/sources:** The psychological effect of delayed resolution is discussed in both formalist and cognitive traditions; the emphasis on expectation vs. structure varies.
- **Source:** Huron, D. (2006). *Sweet Anticipation: Music and the Psychology of Expectation*. MIT Press; Meyer, L. B. (1956). *Emotion and Meaning in Music*. University of Chicago Press.

### Formal contrast

- **Term:** Formal contrast
- **Aliases:** sectional contrast, formal differentiation
- **Category:** arrangement/form-structure
- **Definition:** The deliberate differentiation of sections or passages by density, register, texture, space, or layering, so that they are perceived as distinct formal states.
- **Audible effect:** A clear sense of "change of place" or "new section" without relying on labels.
- **When to use:** When sections need to be distinguished; when contrast serves the form's identity and trajectory.
- **When to avoid / limitations:** When continuity or gradual transformation is the intent; when contrast would fragment a through-composed or accumulative form. Avoid contrast for its own sake or as a default between "verse" and "chorus."
- **Compatible combinations:** Transition as formal event, climax preparation, repetition with formal variation.
- **Conflicts:** Continuity, gradual transformation, accumulative forms where contrast would break the flow.
- **Relation to other concepts:** A formal decision about differentiation; distinct from interpretive contrast (Performance) and from global contrast (Creative Direction).
- **Knowledge type:** consolidated.
- **Source:** Berry, W. (1976). *Structural Functions in Music*. Prentice-Hall.

### Repetition with formal variation

- **Term:** Repetition with formal variation
- **Aliases:** formal variation, varied repetition, returning section variation
- **Category:** arrangement/form-structure
- **Definition:** A returning section or passage that preserves its identity while varying at the formal level (layers, density, register, or texture), so that repetition carries forward motion.
- **Audible effect:** Recognition of the returning material combined with a sense of change or development.
- **When to use:** When a returning section (or loop) needs to develop without losing identity; when variation at the formal level serves the composition.
- **When to avoid / limitations:** When exact repetition is the intended effect (exact repetition is valid and must not be "corrected"). Avoid variation that obscures identity or adds change without a musical reason. Avoid duplicating lyric-level variation (Lyrics) or interpretive variation (Performance) — this entry concerns formal-level change only.
- **Compatible combinations:** Formal contrast, non-conventional forms, climax preparation.
- **Conflicts:** Exact repetition as intended effect; stability as artistic goal.
- **Relation to other concepts:** Formal-level variation only. Lyric changes belong to Lyrics; interpretive changes belong to Performance. A Performance tag cannot create formal variation, and a lyric change does not by itself constitute formal variation. Margulis supports the psychological role of repetition in musical engagement; the formal variation strategy is an application of that insight, not a claim directly established by that source. Berry supports repetition and contrast as structural functions.
- **Knowledge type:** consolidated (repetition psychology and structural functions); interpretation (formal variation strategy).
- **Source:** Margulis, E. H. (2013). *On Repeat: How Music Plays the Mind*. Oxford University Press; Berry, W. (1976). *Structural Functions in Music*. Prentice-Hall.

### Non-conventional forms

- **Term:** Non-conventional forms
- **Aliases:** non-standard forms, through-composed, episodic form, accumulative form, circular form
- **Category:** arrangement/form-structure
- **Definition:** Formal organizations that do not follow a conventional sectional pattern (for example, through-composed, episodic, accumulative, or circular structures), used as possibilities rather than templates.
- **Audible effect:** A form whose identity comes from its own logic (accumulation, transformation, or continuity) rather than from familiar sectional contrast.
- **When to use:** When the composition's intent calls for a form not based on conventional sectional contrast; when accumulation, transformation, or continuity serves the work.
- **When to avoid / limitations:** When the intent is better served by recognizable sectional contrast or by stability. Avoid presenting non-conventional forms as superior or as defaults. Avoid genre-specific form recipes. Avoid forcing a label where none adds value.
- **Compatible combinations:** Repetition with formal variation, delayed resolution, formal contrast (when compatible).
- **Conflicts:** Conventional sectional contrast (when that is the intent); forms requiring clear sectional identity.
- **Relation to other concepts:** A set of possibilities, not a taxonomy or hierarchy. The specific form emerges from the composition's intent and material. The list of possibilities is an interpretive application of formal theory, not a consolidated taxonomy supported by a single source.
- **Knowledge type:** interpretation (possibilities derived from formal theory); convention (genre-specific usage).
- **Variation across traditions/sources:** The valuation of conventional vs. non-conventional form varies across traditions and repertoires; no single form type is universal.
- **Source:** Lerdahl, F., & Jackendoff, R. (1983). *A Generative Theory of Tonal Music*. MIT Press.

## Domain boundaries (reminder)

- **Arrangement library**: formal architecture; does not redefine rhythmic theory (Rhythm), production mechanisms (Production), global sonic identity (Style), vocal interpretation (Performance), or lyric content (Lyrics).
- Rhythm defines metric and groove phenomena; Arrangement materializes them in form and layers.
- Production defines texture mechanisms; Arrangement distributes them per section.
- Style owns global production direction; Arrangement owns per-section texture distribution.

## Consultation policy (reminder)

- Arrangement consults `references/library/arrangement/` when formal decisions are material (creating/changing/reviewing form, sections, order, durations, transitions, climax, repetition/variation, non-conventional forms).
- Pure layer, density, counterpoint, or transition revisions without formal change do not automatically require it.
- Consultation is mandatory when the relevant formal decision is in scope; using a retrieved concept is not (depends on musical adequacy).
- The library provides reference knowledge and never overrides `references/instructions/arrangement.md`.
- See `references/library/README.md` for the full utilization policy.
